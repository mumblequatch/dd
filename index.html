Understanding Dopamine Dysregulation

One Person's Path to Diagnosis

For years, I suffered depression and anxiety and then had a manic episode, which caused my psychiatrist to upgrade my diagnosis from "depression" to "manic depression." I started lithium, a mood stabilizer, and Effexor, which has effects on serotonin, norepinephrine and dopamine.

But the cocktail didn't work. Despite taking three medications, I continued to experience brain fog, cognitive slowing, fatigue, lack of motivation, listlessness, and anhedonia. All of this only intensified in the aftermath of COVID, for reasons that only became clear years later.

What finally broke through was Ritalin. Within thirty minutes of my first dose, the brain fog lifted—the first time in years I'd been without it. I had begun to worry I'd never find a way to address it. Of course, stimulants work primarily by increasing dopamine.

That response functioned as an empirical diagnosis: if stimulants worked when nothing else did, maybe the problem was dopamine.

Around the same time, a cardiologist happened to order ferritin levels. They came back at 23 ng/mL—extremely low. A neurologist told me anyone with neurological complaints should have ferritins at 75–100 ng/mL minimum. Iron is a cofactor for tyrosine hydroxylase, the rate-limiting enzyme in dopamine synthesis. Without adequate iron, you cannot manufacture dopamine at normal rates.

I'd been requiring IV iron infusions (five sessions) every two to three months, which is far too frequent. The question was why I couldn't hold onto iron. The answer turned out to be SIBO—small intestinal bacterial overgrowth—which had been impairing iron absorption for years.

After treating the SIBO with antibiotics followed by an herbal protocol, my ferritin levels began to persist. I now track ferritin proactively to schedule infusions before levels drop too low rather than chasing deficiency.

For ongoing dopamine support, I take pramipexole, a dopamine agonist that directly stimulates D2/D3 receptors. I also use stimulants on a PRN basis when I need additional clarity.

The combined effect of resolving SIBO, maintaining stable ferritin, and directly supporting dopamine has led to substantially greater mental health, cognitive acuity, and overall balance than I'd experienced in years.

The HDR Reward System

Think of dopamine like a camera's dynamic range. Most brains operate like a standard camera: moderate baseline exposure, moderate response to highlights and shadows. But some brains work more like HDR photography: compressed baseline (darker shadows) but amplified response to peaks (brighter highlights).

This is your brain on low tonic dopamine with high phasic dopamine. The technical version: your baseline dopamine is low—probably because overactive dopamine transporters are sucking it out of the synaptic cleft too fast. But your burst dopamine, the phasic response to novel or rewarding stimuli, is actually higher than normal. Low tonic dopamine disinhibits the phasic system.

What this means in practice: routine feels impossible, but novelty is electrifying. You can't make yourself do the boring thing, but give you something genuinely interesting and you'll hyperfocus for six hours without eating. You're not lazy. You're not lacking willpower. Your brain literally does not have enough baseline fuel to run on idle—but it has exceptional acceleration when something actually engages it.

This is why stimulants work. They don't give you more motivation or make you "high." They block the transporters that are removing dopamine too aggressively, allowing baseline levels to rise to something closer to normal. The subjective experience isn't "speeding"—it's "oh, this is what it feels like when the engine isn't misfiring."

The goal isn't to eliminate the traits that make your neurology different. The goal is to keep the system fueled enough that you can access the advantages instead of just suffering the disadvantages.

Everything that follows is an attempt at a maintenance manual: what depletes the system, how to test for problems, how to fix them.

Why Dopamine Gets Overlooked

Serotonin dominates the conversation around depression and mood disorders. SSRIs (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors) like Prozac, Zoloft, and Lexapro are first-line treatments and have been heavily marketed for decades. The "chemical imbalance" narrative in popular culture is essentially a serotonin narrative.

This creates a gap. When someone presents with fatigue, low motivation, anhedonia, and concentration problems, the default assumption is often serotonin-based depression, and the default treatment is an SSRI. But SSRIs do little for dopamine. If the underlying issue is dopaminergic, not serotonergic, someone can trial multiple SSRIs with poor results—or even feel worse (more flat, more fatigued, less motivated).

A useful heuristic: anxious, ruminative depression—where you can't stop worrying, where your mind races—often responds to serotonin-based treatment. Flat, amotivational depression with concentration problems—where you can't start things, where nothing sounds appealing—may not. That may be dopamine.

What Dopamine Does

Dopamine is not the "pleasure chemical"—that's a simplification that misleads. It's more accurately described as the wanting and motivation chemical. It governs:

·      Motivation and drive: The push to initiate and sustain goal-directed behavior

·      Attention and focus: Ability to direct and maintain cognitive resources

·      Reward processing: Learning what's worth pursuing

·      Movement: Motor control and initiation

·      Executive function: Planning, working memory, impulse control

Symptoms of Low Dopamine

·      Anhedonia (nothing sounds appealing)

·      Fatigue and low energy

·      Brain fog, cognitive slowing

·      Difficulty initiating tasks ("paralytic procrastination")

·      Lack of motivation even for things you care about

·      Poor concentration and working memory

·      Motor symptoms: tremor, stiffness, slowed movement (in more severe cases)

Causes

Genetic

ADHD heritability is estimated at 74–80%—among the highest of any psychiatric condition. If you have a first-degree relative with ADHD, your risk is 5–10x higher than the general population.

Specific gene variants associated with dopamine function include:

·      DRD4 (dopamine receptor D4)—associated with novelty-seeking

·      DAT1 (dopamine transporter)—affects how quickly dopamine is cleared from synapses

The addiction connection: Dopamine dysregulation creates vulnerability to addiction. Low baseline dopamine means ordinary activities don't provide adequate reward signal—but substances and behaviors that spike dopamine (drugs, gambling, sugar, screens) feel disproportionately compelling. This isn't moral failure; it's neurochemistry. The same low-dopamine brain that can't get started on taxes will light up for anything that provides reliable dopamine release.

Other Causes

·      Iron deficiency: Iron is a cofactor for tyrosine hydroxylase, the rate-limiting enzyme in dopamine synthesis. Low ferritin = impaired dopamine production.

·      Gut malabsorption: SIBO, celiac disease, and other GI conditions can impair absorption of iron and other nutrients needed for dopamine synthesis.

·      Other nutritional deficiencies: B6, B12, folate, vitamin D, protein (tyrosine is the amino acid precursor to dopamine)

·      Chronic high-sugar/high-fat diet

·      Drug use (depletes the system over time)

·      Brain injury

·      Chronic stress (cortisol suppresses dopamine)

·      Sleep deprivation

Diagnosis

No direct test for dopamine levels exists in clinical practice. Diagnosis relies on symptom assessment, medical history, and ruling out mimics.

For depression/ADHD:

·      Clinical interview and symptom scales

·      Developmental history (ADHD symptoms should trace back to childhood)

·      Medication response is itself diagnostic information—improvement on dopaminergic medications suggests dopamine involvement

Ferritin and nutritional levels can and should be tested via blood work.

For Parkinson's: Dopamine transporter imaging is available.

Treatment

For depression with anhedonia/amotivation:

·      Bupropion (norepinephrine and dopamine activity)

·      Pramipexole: A dopamine agonist (D2/D3 receptor) typically prescribed for Parkinson's and restless legs syndrome, but sometimes used off-label for treatment-resistant depression, particularly with prominent anhedonia and low motivation. Targets the dopamine system directly.

For ADHD:

·      Stimulants: methylphenidate (Ritalin, Concerta), amphetamines (Adderall, Vyvanse)

·      Bupropion (sometimes used when stimulants aren't tolerated)

For Parkinson's:

·      Levodopa, dopamine agonists

Addressing underlying causes:

·      Iron supplementation if ferritin is low (IV infusions if oral not tolerated or absorption impaired)

·      Treating gut malabsorption (SIBO, celiac, H. pylori)

·      Correcting other nutritional deficiencies

Exercise:

Aerobic exercise increases dopamine levels in the striatum, hypothalamus, and brainstem. The effect is robust across age and sex.

·      Walking works. Regular walking is associated with decreased overthinking, improved sleep patterns, and enhanced positive thinking—changes consistent with increased dopamine production.

·      Intensity matters. Higher-intensity exercise appears to be more effective at increasing D2 receptor levels, which are associated with motivation. High intensity interval training (HIIT) shows particular benefit for D2 receptors.

·      Skill and complexity add benefit. Exercise requiring coordination, navigation, or problem-solving (complex movement patterns, obstacle navigation) affects frontal-striatal circuits more than simple repetitive aerobic exercise. Gardening, hiking on uneven terrain, or sports would qualify.

·      Consistency matters most. Any sustainable movement practice is better than an optimal one you won't do.

Dopamine precursor supplements (L-tyrosine, mucuna) do not reliably increase brain dopamine and can interfere with diagnosis.

Next Steps if This Resonates

1. Document Your Symptoms

Before any appointment, write down:

·      Specific symptoms and when they started

·      What makes them better or worse

·      Family history of ADHD, depression, addiction, Parkinson's, or movement disorders

·      Current medications and supplements

·      Diet patterns, sleep quality, exercise habits

·      Past medication trials (especially SSRIs) and your response—poor response to SSRIs is relevant information

2. Get Baseline Blood Work

Request from your primary care physician:

·      Ferritin (not just iron—ferritin is the storage form and more informative)

·      Complete iron panel (serum iron, TIBC, transferrin saturation)

·      B12 and folate

·      Vitamin D

·      Thyroid panel (hypothyroidism mimics low dopamine symptoms)

·      Complete metabolic panel

3. Investigate Gut Health if Ferritin Is Low

Low ferritin despite adequate dietary iron suggests malabsorption. Consider testing for:

·      Celiac disease (tTG-IgA antibody)

·      SIBO (breath test)

·      H. pylori

·      General inflammatory markers

4. Pursue ADHD Evaluation if Relevant

If primary symptoms are attention, focus, executive function, impulsivity, or task initiation:

·      Seek formal ADHD assessment from a psychiatrist, neuropsychologist, or qualified psychologist

·      Evaluation includes clinical interview, symptom rating scales, and developmental history

·      Childhood history matters—ADHD is developmental, so symptoms should trace back even if previously undiagnosed or attributed to something else ("daydreamer," "underachiever," "lazy," "not living up to potential")

·      Rule-outs: sleep disorders, anxiety, and thyroid dysfunction can mimic ADHD

Note: Low ferritin is independently associated with ADHD symptom severity. Correcting iron deficiency can improve symptoms and/or medication response.

5. Seek Appropriate Specialist

·      Psychiatrist: Depression not responding to SSRIs; ADHD evaluation and medication management

·      Neuropsychologist: Formal ADHD testing

·      Functional medicine or gastroenterologist: Gut-related causes, malabsorption

·      Neurologist: Movement symptoms (tremor, stiffness, coordination issues)

6. Avoid Self-Treating with Supplements

Dopamine precursors can interfere with testing and diagnosis, and aren't reliably effective. Get the workup first.

A Final Note: You're in Good Company

If you've read this far and recognize yourself in these descriptions, you join a long lineage of people whose brains work differently—and who have used that difference to remarkable effect. Understanding dopamine dysregulation isn't about finding out what's wrong with you. It's about understanding how your particular neurology works so you can build a life that fits it.

Historical Figures Who May Have Had Dopamine Dysregulation

Retrospective diagnosis is inherently speculative—we can't know for certain. But clinical researchers have analyzed historical records and identified patterns consistent with ADHD or related conditions in many accomplished figures:

Leonardo da Vinci — In 2019, Professor Marco Catani, a neuroscientist specializing in ADHD and autism at King's College London, published a paper in the journal Brain concluding that ADHD is "the most convincing and scientifically plausible hypothesis" to explain Leonardo's well-documented difficulties finishing projects. Historical records show Leonardo spent excessive time planning but lacked perseverance to complete work. His contemporaries complained constantly about his failure to deliver on commissions. Giorgio Vasari's biography describes textbook ADHD: "in learning and in the rudiments of letters he would have made great proficiency, if he had not been so variable and unstable, for he set himself to learn many things, and then, after having begun them, abandoned them." Leonardo was also left-handed, likely dyslexic, slept in short cycles rather than consolidated blocks, and showed atypical right-hemisphere language dominance (found in less than 5% of the population but prevalent in ADHD). Catani states: "I hope that the case of Leonardo shows that ADHD is not linked to low IQ or lack of creativity but rather the difficulty of capitalizing on natural talents."

Thomas Edison — Edison is cited more often than any other historical figure as exhibiting classic ADHD behavior. At age seven, after twelve weeks in a one-room schoolhouse, his teacher lost patience with his persistent questioning, fidgeting, and seemingly self-centered behavior, declaring his brains "addled" (scrambled). Edison was expelled. His mother, a former teacher, pulled him from formal education entirely and taught him at home, where his appetite for knowledge flourished. Thom Hartmann, a former psychotherapist and founder of the Hunter School (a residential school for children with ADHD), uses Edison as the archetypal example of ADHD traits in his book The Edison Gene—creativity, impulsiveness, distractibility—channeled into extraordinary achievement when the environment allows it.

Albert Einstein — Researchers have noted Einstein's childhood speech delay (not speaking until age three), his difficulty with rote memorization, his teachers' complaints about inattentiveness, and his pattern of hyperfocusing on topics that interested him while neglecting everything else. His first wife Mileva reported that he would become so absorbed in thought that he'd forget meals, appointments, and basic self-care—classic hyperfocus. Einstein himself described his thinking as visual and intuitive rather than verbal, a cognitive style associated with ADHD and dyslexia.

Nikola Tesla — Tesla exhibited pronounced ADHD and possible OCD traits: photographic memory combined with difficulty completing projects, sensory hypersensitivity (he couldn't tolerate pearls, for instance, and counted his steps compulsively), and a pattern of obsessive work binges followed by collapse.

Historical Writers:

According to Professor Michael Fitzgerald, Henry Marsh Professor of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry at Trinity College Dublin, the following major literary figures exhibited strong evidence of ADHD based on biographical and behavioral analysis:

Lord Byron (1788–1824) — Fitzgerald published a peer-reviewed paper in the Journal of Medical Biography (2001) arguing Byron had ADHD, citing his turbulent school history, criminal activities, impulsivity, and flight from England, alongside his extraordinary hyperfocus that made him "the greatest lyric poet in the English language."

Mark Twain (1835–1910) — Fitzgerald's 2009 book Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder: Creativity, Novelty Seeking and Risk (Nova Science Publishers) discusses Twain, noting that Tom Sawyer and Huckleberry Finn are themselves literary portraits of ADHD.

Charles Dickens — Fitzgerald describes Dickens as "quick, keen, eager, restless and super energetic… also quite obsessive, as well as being extremely controlling and argumentative." Novelist and biographer Jane Smiley, in her literary biography of Dickens, documented that "every stimulus produced an enormous reaction" in Dickens. Fitzgerald notes that Dickens's novels showed "much evidence of imagination, novelty-seeking and sensory-seeking which often go with hyperkinesis." Unlike the stereotype of the solitary writer, Dickens craved constant stimulation—he was famous for exhausting reading tours where he performed his own work to packed theaters, walking twenty miles a day through London, and maintaining a frenetic pace of productivity that included editing magazines while writing serialized novels simultaneously.

Jules Verne — Fitzgerald notes that Verne struggled in school specifically because he had trouble finishing tasks and projects—a hallmark ADHD difficulty. Yet his hyperfocus on science and adventure produced foundational works of science fiction: Twenty Thousand Leagues Under the Sea, Journey to the Center of the Earth, Around the World in Eighty Days. The same mind that couldn't complete conventional schoolwork invented entire genres.
